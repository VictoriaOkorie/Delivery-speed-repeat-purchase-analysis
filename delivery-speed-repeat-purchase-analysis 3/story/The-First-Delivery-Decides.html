The First Delivery Decides

What 96,000 Brazilian online shoppers reveal about the quiet link between a slow parcel and a customer who never comes back, and the 60 days a growth team has to win them again.

By Victoria Okorie · Data analysis, writing and charts · November 2026

Each square is one in every 100 customers. Only 3 of them ever place a second order.

Between the autumn of 2016 and the summer of 2018, roughly 96,000 people bought something through Olist, a company that connects small Brazilian shops to the country's biggest online marketplaces. They bought bed sheets and perfume, phone cases and garden hoses. Most of their orders arrived. Most of them left a review, usually a generous one. And then almost all of them disappeared.

Of every 100 customers in the data, 97 bought exactly once. In growth language, the marketplace was very good at acquiring customers and almost unable to keep them. That is not unusual for a marketplace, where shoppers come for a product rather than a shop. But it makes every returning customer valuable: across the customers I followed, those who came back spent on average 126 reais more over the period than those who bought once.

So I asked a question most sales dashboards never ask. Not "what did customers buy?" but "what happened to them after they bought?" Specifically: does the experience of waiting for that first parcel change whether a person ever buys again?

1A month on the doorstep

The typical first order took about 11 days to arrive. A fifth arrived within a week. But a long tail did not: 2,189 customers in the study group waited more than a month for their very first parcel.

When I split customers by how long that first delivery took, the pattern was hard to miss. The faster the parcel, the more likely the customer was to come back.

Customers whose first order arrived within a week returned at 2.07%. Those who waited more than 30 days returned at 1.10%, close to half the rate. The numbers are small because returning is rare for everyone, but the gap is consistent: each step slower is a step down, and a statistical test confirms the gap between groups is very unlikely to be chance.

Small rates can hide big shifts. Look at it the other way round: a customer delivered within a week was about 1.9 times as likely to come back as one who waited a month.

2It is the wait, not the broken promise

The obvious explanation is broken promises. At checkout, every Olist customer sees an estimated delivery date. Surely the customers who never return are the ones whose parcel arrived after that date?

The data says no, and the reason is one of the more surprising details in the whole dataset. Olist's estimates were extremely cautious. The typical customer was promised delivery in 25 days and received it in 11. 80% of first orders arrived more than a week ahead of the promise. Only about one in twenty arrived late.

Customers did not judge the parcel against the date on the screen. They judged it against their own patience.Finding from a logistic regression on 52,542 customers

To separate the two ideas, I used logistic regression, a method that estimates the effect of one factor while holding others constant. I controlled for the customer's region, the order's value, the share of the price that was shipping, the number of items, the payment method, the product category and the season.

After all of that, each extra week of waiting cut the odds of a return by about 11%. Lateness against the promised date, on its own, was not statistically significant, and once waiting time was in the model it added nothing at all. The same was true of the review score: it captured the customer's frustration, but it did not predict a return any better than the waiting time already did.

3Where the anger goes first

If slow customers do not complain by leaving immediately, where does the frustration show up? In the review. Olist sends a satisfaction survey when the parcel arrives, and the stars fall off a cliff after the third week.

Only 7.5% of customers who received their first parcel within a week gave it one or two stars. Among those who waited more than 30 days it was 63%. For a marketplace, this matters twice: the unhappy customer is less likely to come back, and their public review makes the next stranger less likely to buy at all.

4Geography is not destiny, but it helps

Brazil is enormous, and most Olist sellers are in the Southeast, around São Paulo. A customer in the Northeast waited almost twice as long, on average, as a customer in the Southeast, and came back less often.

That raises a fair question: is it really delivery speed, or just that people in different regions shop differently? This is why the regression controls for region. Even comparing customers within the same region, a longer wait still predicted fewer returns. The effect also holds when I follow every delivered customer, not only the 180-day group, using survival curves that track how many customers have come back by each day after delivery.

5The 60-day window

Knowing who is likely to return is half the job for a growth team. The other half is when. Of the customers who did come back, the median return came about 81 days after the first parcel arrived. But a median hides the shape that matters most for timing a campaign.

The fairer way to look at timing is to ask: of the customers who have not come back yet, what share come back in each month? That is the chance a win-back message is competing for.

The first 30 days after delivery carry a return chance about 2.9 times the long-run monthly level. Days 31 to 60 are still about 1.8 times higher. After roughly day 90, the chance settles into a low, flat trickle of about 0.19% a month and stays there. The customer has not left in a dramatic way. They have simply gone quiet.

The second order is won or lost in the first two months. After that, a customer is not churning. They are forgetting.

6The second order is the real conversion

There is one more reason the window matters. When I followed customers who did come back, their behaviour changed. A first-time customer had about a 1.7% chance of ordering again within 180 days. A customer who had already ordered twice had an 8.2% chance of a third order in the same time.

I also checked whether customers who came back quickly became more loyal than those who came back late. They did not: the chance of a third order was similar whether the second came within 30 days or after 90, and the difference was not statistically significant. The lesson for a marketer is simple. What matters is getting the second order at all, and the first 60 days are when it is easiest to get.

7What a growth team could do on Monday

Put together, the findings point to a playbook that joins two teams that rarely sit together: operations, which owns the parcel, and marketing, which owns the customer.

Before the orderOperations

Protect the first delivery

Route first-time buyers to the fastest sellers and carriers available, especially in the North and Northeast. The first parcel is the one that decides whether there will be a second.

Days 3 to 10Customer experience

Say thank you, ask for the review

A how-to-use note and review request. For any first order that took more than three weeks, add a sincere apology and a small credit toward the next order.

Days 14 to 30Marketing

Send the first real offer

This is the period with the highest chance of return. Personalise it by the first purchase category: bed, bath and table, sports and leisure, fashion accessories and perfumery all bring customers back above the average.

Days 45 to 60Marketing

One stronger reminder

A last, slightly bigger incentive for customers who have not returned, while the window is still open.

After day 90Marketing

Stop spending, keep in touch

The chance of return is low and flat. Move these customers to a low-cost newsletter instead of paid retargeting.

After order twoLifecycle

Treat them as a new segment

Third orders came a median of about 45 days after the second. Plan the next nudge around then, and measure "second order rate" as the main retention goal.

How much is this worth? Honestly, it depends, and the data cannot promise a number. If every customer whose first parcel took more than two weeks had returned at the rate of customers delivered in 8 to 14 days, the study group would have produced about 79 more returning customers, an increase of roughly 8%. On a marketplace where only about 2 in 100 customers return, that is a modest but real gain, and it comes from something a business already controls. The dashboard that comes with this story has a planner where a team can change the assumptions and see the effect.

What this data cannot tell us

  • It shows association, not proof of cause. I controlled for the obvious differences between customers, but something unmeasured could still link slow deliveries and fewer returns. The real test is an experiment: speed up delivery for a random group of first-time buyers and compare.
  • Repeat purchase is rare here. About 1,800 customers returned in total, so some groups, such as late deliveries, are small and their rates are uncertain. I show confidence intervals for that reason.
  • There are no marketing campaigns in the data. The timing recommendations come from natural return behaviour. Any win-back campaign should be tested with a holdout group before it is rolled out.
  • It is one marketplace, in one country, in 2016 to 2018. Expectations about delivery speed have risen since then, which, if anything, makes the first delivery matter more.

Most growth conversations start with acquisition: more traffic, more first orders. This data suggests a quieter, cheaper lever sitting in the warehouse and the delivery van. The first parcel is not the end of the sale. For a new customer, it is the first page of the relationship, and they decide quickly whether there will be a second.

How this was made

Data: Olist Brazilian e-commerce public dataset: 99,441 anonymised orders from 96,096 customers placed between September 2016 and August 2018, released by Olist (also published on Kaggle). Customers were matched across orders with the dataset's unique customer identifier.

Study group: 52,542 customers whose first order was delivered and who could be followed for 180 days after delivery. A "return" is a new, not cancelled order placed after the first parcel arrived. Orders placed before it arrived (mostly same-day split baskets) were not counted.

Methods: Python (pandas) for cleaning, SQLite and Structured Query Language for the analysis queries, chi-square tests, logistic regression with controls, and Kaplan-Meier survival estimates to handle customers followed for different lengths of time. Full code, queries and notebook are in the project repository.
